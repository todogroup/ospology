# Open source and digital sovereignty

The International Criminal Court decided to switch from relying on Microsoft Office for its internal work environment to OpenDesk in the wake of an episode in which Microsoft appeared to cancel the email account of the chief prosecutor, Karim Khan. Microsoft denies the claims, but given the repeated threats from President Donald Trump against Khan, the ICC felt uncomfortable continuing to rely on an American technology company (https://www.euractiv.com/news/international-criminal-court-to-ditch-microsoft-office-for-european-open-source-alternative/). 

More recently, the US government forced Anthropic to close access to their latest model to non-US-citizens, leading the company to pull access for everyone, worldwide. 

At one point in the past, coal and steel were so important for economies that countries placed strict limits on the import of both and subsidized local production so that in case of war, there would still be enough steel produced inside the country to meet the country’s needs. 

But the examples above illustrate the dangers of relying on digital infrastructure that is located in and subject to the political and legal regime of another country. Not only could it be simply turned off if the political differences become too large, the sharing of government, company and/or personal data with a foreign country has privacy implications that many jurisdictions find unacceptable.. If a European municipality relies on US-based tech companies to communicate with residents, will it be unable to do so if the U.S. government decides that tech companies can’t operate outside of the U.S.? If a European government relies on a major hyperscaler to manage its daily work, will it still be able to function in the unlikely event of a war with the U.S.?

Open source software is a hedge against this kind of scenario, which is a risk factor not just for using technology from major hyperscalers but from all SaaS subscriptions from US-based companies. Open source software, first of all, is run locally. If you run the software locally, it can’t be ‘turned off,’ so even if the team that maintains the software is primarily based in a country that turns ‘hostile,’ you would be able to continue to use the software without any disruptions. 

This is the case for other types of software you run on-prem as well. The difference is that with open source software, you will be able to create your own internal fork. Yes, you’d rather have access to the maintenance team, but if you need to you can maintain the software on your own, fix bugs and security vulnerabilities yourself, and advance the project on your own. This makes you considerably less vulnerable in case of geopolitical problems. 

When governments talk about digital sovereignty, there’s often an emphasis on using software that’s made inside the country’s national borders. In practice, this can be extremely limiting and hard to prove, and force governments to use software that lacks the functionality or ease-of-use of market leaders. 

Open source software, regardless of where the core maintainer team is located, gives software users the freedom to continue using the software no matter what happens geo-politically. You can maintain the software yourself if needed.
